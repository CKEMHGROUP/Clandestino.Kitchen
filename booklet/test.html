About

A word about the dining experience “a Culinary Journey”.

Our signature tasting menu is a multi-course sensory dinner inspired by Joe's travels, experiences, and personal history in a variety of starred kitchens. Drawing on his origins and the influence of his grandparents during his childhood, it embodies his core beliefs in gastronomy and its fundamentals, which he describes as “food culture.”

The basis of our concept is a one table fine dining experience, with quality food and exceptional hand selected wine pairings.

An experience often shared with strangers, foodies from all over the world in very often changing locations masked as popup events. Dynamics and energy change therefore immediately when entering the space. Which is intended by elevating a dining experience to its major meaning.

To recognize the importance of food culture, which connects us all, is to grasp the very essence of what it means to be human. It transcends our differences and binds us together.

Therefore, Joe pours great care into every dish, each one crafted from a story behind a person, a place, or a historical curiosity.

Tony's First Oyster

A moment in France that sparked a lifelong culinary passion.

Behind the dish

During a family trip to France, a young Anthony Bourdain tasted his first oyster fresh from the sea. It was a transformative experience—a briny, metallic, and profoundly adult flavor that awakened his senses. This single moment ignited a relentless curiosity for food, culture, and the human stories behind every meal, setting him on the path to becoming one of the world's most beloved culinary storytellers.

Vichyssoise

A cold soup masked as French

Behind the dish

In the summer of 1917, Louis Diat, a French expat, invented this dish in New York at the Ritz-Carlton. He was a former apprentice of Auguste Escoffier, the undisputed kingpin of French cuisine. Nowadays, a dish that is often served in the finest restaurants of the world. People still believe its origin is French. But no French chef ever heard in 500 years of culinary arts of a French dish called “Vichyssoise”. Louis Diat invented this dish in the United States because in that epoch it was “chic” to be French. A worldwide known classic disguised as French but of American origin.

Green Tomato

The “Spanish Nigiri”—an entire nation's flavor in a single bite.

Behind the dish

Just as a single piece of nigiri can perfectly encapsulate the essence of Japanese culinary philosophy—precision, balance, and respect for the ingredient—this dish aims to do the same for Spain. Chef Joe's signature creation is a study in contrasts: the fresh acidity of green tomato, the rich depth of Iberian ham, and the smoky whisper of paprika. It's a bold distillation of Spain's vibrant, sun-drenched terroir, designed to transport you to its landscapes in one perfect bite.

Joe Dog's “Mobsters” Meatball

A recipe born in crime, loyalty, and betrayal.

Behind the dish

Joe “Dogs” Iannuzzi wasn't just a mobster; he was the personal cook for the NY Gambino crime family. His meatballs, slow-simmered in a rich tomato sauce rumored to be a favorite of Frank Sinatra, were legendary in the inner circles of the New York mafia. This dish is a taste of that history—a classic Italo-New Yorker recipe that speaks of backroom deals and family loyalty, before Iannuzzi traded his ladle for an FBI wire and documented his life in his book “The Mafia Cookbook.”

Bife á Café “Marraré”

Lisbon's most iconic steak, born in the 19th century.

Behind the dish

In the 1800s, Italian immigrant Antonio Marrare opened a café in Lisbon that would become legendary. The “Café Marrare” was famed for its bohemian clientele and, above all, for its signature steak: Bife á Café. A tender steak cooked in a rich, coffee-infused cream sauce, it became the talk of the city and a nationwide sensation. Though the original café is long gone, its most famous dish remains an icon of Lisbon's culinary heritage, a symbol of a bygone era of intellectual and artistic fervor.

Tarantino's Apfelstrudel

A dessert of unbearable tension and cinematic history.

Behind the dish

The Apfelstrudel, a delicate pastry of South Tyrol, takes center stage in one of cinema's most suspenseful scenes. In Quentin Tarantino's “Inglourious Basterds,” the seemingly innocent act of ordering this dessert becomes a masterclass in tension. The meticulous ordering, the dollop of cream, the pause before the first bite, each moment is loaded with unspoken threat. This dish pays homage to that scene, capturing the contrast between homely comfort and chilling suspense.
